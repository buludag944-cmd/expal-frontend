When people imagine moving to Ireland, they often begin with the destination: Dublin’s international companies, Cork’s growing industries, Galway’s energy or the possibility of building a different life abroad.

But the first question should not be, “How do I move to Ireland?”

It should be: “Does Ireland make sense for my career?”

Relocation becomes much easier when it is built around a realistic professional opportunity. Before investing months in applications—or making expensive plans—use the following checklist to test whether Ireland fits your experience, goals and circumstances.

1. Research your profession, not just the country

Begin with the exact roles you could realistically apply for today. Search Irish job boards and company career pages for several versions of your job title. Read at least 20 current job descriptions and look for patterns:

Which skills appear repeatedly?

How many years of experience do employers request?

Which tools, qualifications or certifications are common?

Are roles concentrated in Dublin, or are there opportunities elsewhere?

Is the work usually on-site, hybrid or remote?

Your goal is not simply to prove that vacancies exist. It is to understand whether your profile matches the vacancies that exist—and what you would need to strengthen before applying.

Create a simple skills table with three columns: “I have this,” “I can demonstrate this” and “I need to develop this.” The distinction matters. Employers do not only look for a skill on a CV; they look for evidence that you have used it to achieve something.

2. Understand your work-permission position early

Your job-search strategy depends heavily on whether you already have the right to work in Ireland.

Irish, UK, EU, EEA and Swiss citizens have a different position from non-EEA nationals who may need an employment permit. Ireland has several permit routes, including the Critical Skills Employment Permit and the General Employment Permit. Eligibility can depend on the occupation, proposed salary, employer and other conditions.

Do not assume that a job title is eligible because a similar role qualified in the past. Occupation lists, salary thresholds and rules can change. Check the current Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment guidance before building your application strategy.

If you need sponsorship, focus on employers and roles where hiring international talent is realistic. Be direct about your status, but do not let it become the opening sentence of your professional story. Lead with the value you offer, then explain the permission or permit required clearly.

3. Compare salary with the life it needs to support

A salary can look attractive until it meets rent, transport, utilities and the cost of setting up a new home.

Estimate your likely monthly take-home pay, then build a practical budget for the city where the jobs are located. Include:

Temporary accommodation on arrival

Rent and deposit

Electricity, heating and internet

Public transport or car costs

Groceries and personal expenses

Immigration or registration costs, where relevant

An emergency buffer

Avoid using a national average as your only reference. A role in Dublin may offer a higher salary but also involve higher housing costs. A position elsewhere may offer a different balance between income, commuting and quality of life.

The useful question is not, “Is this a good salary?” It is, “Would this salary support the life I expect in this location?”

4. Test the market before committing to the move

You do not need to wait until you feel completely ready. Run a small, structured experiment.

Over four weeks:

Adapt your CV to Irish hiring conventions.

Apply to a focused group of well-matched roles.

Contact recruiters working in your field.

Connect with professionals in Ireland who hold similar positions.

Record applications, replies, interviews and recurring feedback.

Ten thoughtful applications can teach you more than 100 generic ones. If employers consistently respond, your profile may already have traction. If they do not, examine the pattern: unclear positioning, missing local terminology, a skills gap, location, seniority or work authorization.

Treat silence as data, not as a verdict on your ability.

5. Look beyond the first job

The first offer can feel like the finish line, but it is only the beginning. Ask what Ireland could offer your career over the next three to five years.

Consider:

Are there several employers in your field, or would you depend on one company?

Can you progress without leaving the country?

Will the experience strengthen your international profile?

Are professional communities, events or qualifications available?

Does the lifestyle fit the person you want to become—not only the employee you want to be?

A move is more resilient when it creates options. Ideally, Ireland should offer not just one job, but an environment in which your career can continue to grow.

Your Ireland career-fit checklist

Before deciding, make sure you can answer these questions:

What exact roles will I target?

Which Irish employers hire for them?

Does my current profile match their recurring requirements?

Do I already have permission to work, or might I need an employment permit?

Is my expected salary workable in my target location?

What evidence suggests I can secure interviews?

What would career progress look like after the first role?

You do not need perfect certainty. You need enough reliable information to make the next decision with confidence.
